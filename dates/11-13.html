A Desert Intersection

In the Tarim Basin in western China, a curved ridge and winding river converge in a Y-shaped tangle of sand, rock, and water channels. Even from space, it’s easy to see why the colorful ridge—Mazartagh—is known in Chinese as Hongbaishan, or “red-white mountain.”

The OLI-2 (Operational Land Imager-2) on Landsat 9 acquired this image on September 11, 2025, showing where the river meets the ridge. The wall-like ridge stretches 145 kilometers (90 miles) to the northwest and rises about 200 meters (600 feet) above the surrounding desert. It acts as a natural barrier, trapping sand and blocking dunes from moving southward under the basin’s frequent northeasterly winds.

The dark red rock formations on the northern side of Mazartagh are made of iron-rich sandstones and mudstones, likely deposited in an arid, oxygen-rich environment such as a desert dune field or alluvial fan. In contrast, the lighter-colored formations in the southern part of the ridge are composed of layers of gypsum and calcareous sandstone, which typically form in shallow water environments, such as lake beds.

A broader satellite image shows the eastern edge of the Takla Makan Desert in the Tarim Basin. A reddish ridge labeled Mazartagh runs diagonally across the center, separating different parts of the desert. To the left and below, long linear dunes dominate, while to the right, transverse dunes are visible near the ridge. The dark green, branching Hotan River (also called the Khotan River) flows along the right side of the image, contrasting sharply with the tan desert sands.

The Hotan River is a seasonal waterway fed by glacial meltwater from the Kunlun Mountains to the south. It carries large summer flows northward across the Takla Makan Desert until it meets the Tarim River in the far northern basin. Of the many glacial meltwater streams from the mountains, the Hotan is the only one sustaining a long-distance flow in one of the world’s driest deserts. Vegetation along the river includes poplars, salt cedars, and seasonal grasses, which thrive despite harsh saline soils and variable water availability.

Both Mazartagh and the Hotan River influence the surrounding dune fields. Mazartagh blocks about 62 percent of the sand and reduces north-to-south drift potential of dunes by 13 percent. North of the ridge, sand seas accumulate into barchan, compound transverse, and star dunes. South of the ridge, linear and compound linear dunes dominate, though notches in the ridge allow some barchan fields to form in that area as well.

The region’s landscape and geology have shaped human activity. The Hotan River carries sediments rich in both white and green nephrite jade. People have long collected these precious minerals from riverbeds, making the area a key stop along the Silk Road.

Part of an ancient fort dating to the eighth century, during the Tibetan Empire, still stands atop a hill overlooking the river. In 1907, a British archaeologist excavated the site, Mazar Tagh, uncovering hundreds of military documents that helped reveal the region’s early history.
